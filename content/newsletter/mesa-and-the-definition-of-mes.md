On 30 June 2026, MESA International ceased operation. The same day, the International Society of Automation (ISA) took on its content and its community.

It wasn't a merger in the usual sense. MESA wound down, and ISA agreed to become the home for what it had built.

MESA gave the industry its most quoted definition of MES and a common language for scoping projects. The most useful part of that legacy is a list from 1997, and you can put it to work this week.

## The MESA-11 model

MESA described MES as a "dynamic information system that drives effective execution of manufacturing operations". Then it set out what that means in eleven functions:

1. Operations and detailed sequencing
2. Releasing production units to the shopfloor
3. Product tracking and genealogy
4. Labour management
5. Quality management
6. Resource allocation and status
7. Document control
8. Performance analysis, OEE included
9. Process management
10. Maintenance management
11. Data collection and acquisition

Two things stand out when you read that list today.

**It's far broader than production execution.** Quality, maintenance, labour and genealogy are all in there. That breadth is what we now call MOM.

**It's a functional map, not a product specification.** It describes what a factory needs managed, whatever does the managing.

## Use it as a scoping checklist

That second point makes MESA-11 a useful tool, even today. Go down the list and, next to each function, write what handles it in your factory right now. ERP, MES, a spreadsheet, a whiteboard, or nobody.

You'll usually find a few things:

- Some functions are covered twice, by systems that don't agree with each other
- Some live in one person's head or one person's spreadsheet
- Some aren't really managed at all
- Some handovers between functions still rely on re-keying

That page is the start of your scope. It also gives you a far better conversation with any vendor than asking whether their product "does MES".

## What changes now ISA has it

Probably less than you'd think, at least day to day. ISA-95 and ISA-88 already underpinned much of MESA's educational work, so the content has moved to the organisation that owns the standards it was built on.

## Configure, don't customise

One point MESA made in recent years deserves to outlive it: MES systems should be configurable and largely out of the box, because heavy customisation makes them expensive to deploy and painful to upgrade.

## My view

I'd like to see MESA's practical, manufacturer-led material sitting right alongside ISA-95, so people learning MES get the standard and the real-world guidance in one place. And I strongly agree with MESA's point about configuration over customisation.

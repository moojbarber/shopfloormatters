Two vendors can both call their product an MES and be selling very different things.

One means the production pillar: release the order, run it, record counts, scrap and downtime, calculate OEE. The other means a platform that runs production, quality, inventory and maintenance on the same data. Same three letters on the brochure, very different factory once it's live.

So when you're buying, the label tells you very little.

## Where the confusion comes from

MES is the original term. Early systems focused on the production process, and that's what "execution system" described.

Over time, many of them grew. They took on quality, inventory and traceability, maintenance, logistics, tooling, energy and skills. At that point the old name didn't fit any more, and MOM, manufacturing operations management, emerged as the more accurate one. ISA-95 defines its scope as four areas: production, quality, inventory and maintenance.

MES is one part of MOM. Plenty of people still use the two words interchangeably.

## The real decision: one platform, or one system per pillar?

The alternative to a MOM platform is a separate system for each pillar, integrated together: an OEE tool, a quality system, inventory in ERP and a CMMS for maintenance. Each can be bought on its own timeline, which is why so many factories end up there.

The trade-offs are worth knowing before you start:

- **Context is lost at each integration.** Usually only a subset of the data passes across, so the quality system may not know which order was running.
- **Integrations need maintaining.** They need retesting with every upgrade on either side.
- **Operators may juggle applications,** with different logins and screens for related tasks.
- **Data can diverge.** Two systems can hold different versions of the same downtime or stock.

## Four questions to ask any vendor

Next time you're in a demo, ask these:

1. Which of production, quality, inventory and maintenance do you cover natively?
2. How deep does each one go? Show me, don't tell me.
3. Do they share one data model, or are they separate products integrated together?
4. What does the operator see: one application, or several?

The answers tell you far more than whether the brochure says MES or MOM. For question 4, ask to see a full shift from the operator's screen.

## My view

A good MOM platform replaces the need for a separate QMS or CMMS. You may still need an EAM or a LIMS, depending on your industry and regulations. Dedicated systems should be deliberate choices about depth, with clear boundaries. What I'd avoid is drifting into four point solutions because each one fixed a single problem quickly.

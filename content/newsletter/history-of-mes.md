In 1969, General Motors put the first programmable logic controller to work. It replaced banks of hard-wired relays with a computer that could be reprogrammed.

That one change set off fifty years of factory systems, each built on the one before. Knowing that story makes it much easier to see where your own factory is, and where AI fits.

## How we got here

**Machines first.** After the PLC, automation spread quickly. SCADA let engineers monitor and adjust equipment from a central screen, and process plants adopted distributed control systems. These control systems are still the foundation of every modern factory.

**Then planning.** On the business side, MRP worked out what to make and buy, and when. It grew into MRP II, then ERP, which brought finance and planning onto one set of data.

That left a gap between the plan in ERP and the machines on the floor.

**So every department filled its own part of the gap.** Quality bought a quality system and maintenance bought a maintenance system. Each one solved a real problem. Few of them shared data with each other.

**MES brought it together.** The term emerged in the early 1990s. MESA published its eleven-function model in 1997, and in 2000 the first part of ISA-95 defined the four areas of manufacturing operations: production, quality, inventory and maintenance. As platforms grew to cover all four, the term MOM came into use.

**Then everything got connected.** Since the 2010s, Industry 4.0 has pushed manufacturers towards connected equipment and analytics. Modern MES/MOM platforms connect to IIoT devices and data platforms, and increasingly build AI into the tools operators and engineers use every day.

## Find your factory on the timeline

Be honest about which of these describes your shopfloor today:

1. **People and paper.** Quality and coordination depend on experienced people and handwritten records.
2. **Automated machines, manual data.** The equipment is controlled by PLCs, but production data still goes on paper or into spreadsheets.
3. **Departmental systems.** Quality, maintenance and production each have their own system, and none of them agree.
4. **One operations platform.** Production, quality, inventory and maintenance work from the same data.
5. **Connected and data-driven.** Equipment feeds the platform automatically, and AI is part of daily work.

Most factories today sit somewhere between paper-based and partly digital. That's normal, and it isn't a reason to wait.

## What history says about AI

Each step built on the data the step before created. Control systems made automation possible. MES/MOM later brought shopfloor data together with its context.

AI is the next step, with one difference: it doesn't have to wait its turn. Models that predict and optimise operations learn from reliable, contextualised data, and they're one of the strongest reasons to build it. From here, the data foundation and the AI grow together.

## My view

The history of MES is a story of scope growing, from recording production to running the whole operation as MOM. AI builds on the same data, and gives every factory a new reason to capture it well. I expect the two to grow together.
